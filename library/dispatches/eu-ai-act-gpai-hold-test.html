A year into the EU's AI rules for model makers, Brussels has yet to fine anyone

The duties on every general-purpose model provider took effect in August 2025, and the Commission's own power to fine them, which arrived a year later, has so far produced one round of information requests to more than 30 providers.

5 min read2026-09-18

What "in force" has meant since August 2025

Since 2 August 2025, a company that places a general-purpose AI model on the EU market has carried four legal duties, whatever the model is called. The AI Act defines that model as one trained "with a large amount of data using self-supervision at scale" that "displays significant generality" and "can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications."1

Four duties, whatever the provider

Those four duties are documentation, disclosure, copyright, and transparency. A provider must draw up and keep current technical documentation for the AI Office and national authorities to inspect on request (Article 53(1)(a)).1 It must give downstream AI-system builders the documentation to understand and use it (Article 53(1)(b)).1 It must adopt a policy for complying with EU copyright law, including honoring the rights creators reserve against training use (Article 53(1)(c)).1 And, to an AI Office template, it must publish a "sufficiently detailed summary" of what the model was trained on (Article 53(1)(d)).1

The first two duties fall away for a model released under a free and open license with public parameters, weights, and architecture, unless it has been classified as posing a systemic risk (Article 53(2)).1

The Act itself sets no compute figure for this base tier. The Commission's own guidance puts the practical line at 10^23 floating-point operations of training compute, paired with the ability to generate text, audio, images, or video.2 That is enough, it says, to train a model with about a billion parameters on a large dataset, though the line "is not an absolute rule." A company that fine-tunes someone else's model becomes a provider bound by these duties only when that run uses more than a third of the original training compute.2

A second tier with no name on its list

A heavier set of duties attaches once a model crosses into "systemic risk," a designation the Act reserves for capabilities that "match or exceed the capabilities recorded in the most advanced general-purpose AI models" (Article 3(64)).1 The Act presumes that risk once a training run has used more than 10^25 floating-point operations of compute, a threshold that costs tens of millions of euros to reach, per an estimate the Commission cites.3 A provider that crosses that line has two weeks to tell the Commission (Article 52(1)), though it may still argue the model poses no systemic risk (Article 52(2)).1

Once notified, or designated by the Commission on its own initiative, a provider takes on four further duties (Article 55(1)).1 It must evaluate the model with state-of-the-art adversarial testing, and assess and mitigate the risks it poses across the EU. It must also report serious incidents to the AI Office without delay, and secure the model and its infrastructure against attack.

The Act also requires the Commission to publish and keep current a list of every model designated this way (Article 52(6)).1 No such list is public, and no source names a model as notified or designated. The gap means the record is incomplete, not that the tier is empty: a provider that crosses the threshold has no reason to announce it before the Commission does.

Twenty-one signed. Meta refused.

The Commission published a General-Purpose AI Code of Practice on 10 July 2025, in three chapters. Transparency and Copyright are open to any provider and aimed at Article 53. Safety and Security binds only the systemic-risk tier under Article 55.4 Signing does not by itself prove compliance, only a future harmonised standard carries that legal presumption (Article 53(4)),1 but the Commission calls it a route to "more legal certainty and trust" than proving compliance other ways.4

Twenty-one companies signed the full code, Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI among them; xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter, leaving its Article 53 compliance to another route.4 Meta did not sign. Its global affairs chief, Joel Kaplan, wrote on LinkedIn that the code went "far beyond the scope of the AI Act" and would "stunt" European AI growth, a week after OpenAI had committed to sign it.5

One year of duties, one round of questions

None of this could cost a provider money until 2 August 2026, when the Act's fining power took effect, a year after Chapter V's duties did (Article 113; Article 101).1 From that date the AI Office can demand information from a provider, evaluate a model directly, or order a fix, including pulling the model from the market. It can also fine a provider up to 3 percent of its global annual turnover or 15 million euros, whichever is higher, for violating these duties, defying a fix, or lying to an information request.3 Requests for information are the least aggressive tool on that list, and it is the only one used since.

The Commission announced the start of its enforcement power on 31 July 2026 without naming a provider, and named none in the month after.6 The only enforcement action on record is a first round of information requests to more than 30 AI providers on 1 September 2026, split between a safety-and-security strand for the most advanced models and a second aimed at providers that had stayed out of informal compliance talks.7 No fine, no ordered fix, and no model evaluation has been reported since.

A model already on the EU market before 2 August 2025 has until 2 August 2027 to meet any of these duties (Article 111(3)).1 The models readers already use, if placed on the market before that date, still have another year to catch up.
