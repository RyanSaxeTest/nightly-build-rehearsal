# Voice guide: dispatches/eu-ai-act-gpai-one-year-on (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a short explanatory article for a reader who follows AI and policy,
knows what a language model is, and has never opened Regulation 2024/1689. It
holds the house register, a serious paper, because the press has no voice of
its own yet. Every term the Act coins, starting with "general-purpose AI model"
itself, gets its meaning in the sentence that introduces it, and nothing is
explained twice.

Kerr's context paragraph shows the opening move available here. He says what
the statute does in one plain sentence, quotes the two statutory phrases the
whole dispute turns on, and then asks the questions a reader would ask, in the
reader's own words. For the provider obligations in Article 53 and the
systemic-risk tier in Articles 51 and 55, the same move is open: the Act's own
phrase where the exact words decide something, the writer's ordinary words
everywhere else, and the reader's questions asked before they are answered.

Keller's paragraph on Articles 15, 17 and 18 shows how a set of duties can read
as paragraphs with the article numbers carried inline. Each duty gets a
sentence, the article number sits at the end in parentheses, and the regulation
is quoted only for the one phrase that matters. Her closing parenthetical
answers the question a platform would ask first, who pays. Where the piece lays
out what a provider must document, hand downstream, publish, and adopt, that
shape is available, and the detail worth a parenthetical is the one a
provider's lawyer would ask about.

Kerr's "one way to read" paragraph shows how to separate what a text settled
from what it left open, and how to mark the writer's own reading as a reading,
with reasons and with the writer's stake disclosed. The question in the
commission, which duties bind today and which have teeth yet, may call for the
same separation: what the Regulation fixes by date, what the Commission's
guidelines and the Code of Practice add, and what nobody has yet exercised.
Where the record is silent, the piece can say so plainly and move on.

Keller's cost paragraph shows analysis built one consequence at a time from a
fact the reader can see, each sentence following from the one before it. If the
piece explains why a provider signed the Code or refused it, or what the grace
period means for a model already on the market, that chain is available, and it
stops where the sources stop. Her findings about the DSA are hers about the
DSA. The passages are here for the writing.

Near 800 words, the piece has room for two or three of these moves. The one it
cannot skip is the plain statement of the duties.

## Orin Kerr, "The Supreme Court Reins In the CFAA in Van Buren"

Source: https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/supreme-court-reins-cfaa-van-buren

> "First, some context. The CFAA criminalizes unauthorized access to a
> computer. For years, the big question raised by the CFAA is what counts as an
> unauthorized access. The statute speaks of two ways of violating the statute
> -- "access without authorization" and "exceed[ing] authorized access" -- but
> the cases had not drawn sharp distinctions between them and the government's
> briefs often had just spoken of 'unauthorized access' as an undifferentiated
> whole. And the basic concept of authorization was largely up for grabs. What
> makes an access unauthorized? Is that hacking in? Is that violating terms of
> service? Or just visiting a computer in circumstances the computer owner
> wouldn't like?"

He names what the statute does in one sentence, quotes the two statutory
phrases the dispute turns on, brackets and all, and then puts the open question
as three possibilities in words a non-lawyer already has: hacking in, breaking
terms of service, visiting where the owner would rather you did not. The person
is in that last list. A summary of the case law would not have chosen those
three examples or put them as questions.

> "One way to read Van Buren -- not the only way, but the way that seems most
> plausible to me at this point -- is that it does the major conceptual work of
> reining in the CFAA by casting it properly as a trespass statute. It now
> leaves to lower courts the largely interstitial work of figuring out the hard
> line-drawing of what exactly counts as enough of a closed gate to trigger
> liability. The authentication test suggested in Footnote 9 is one way to do
> it. And I personally tend to think it's the right way; for what it's worth,
> it's the test I argued for in Norms of Computer Trespass. But whatever the
> specific right answer is, the Court has now directed lower courts to the
> right question."

He gives his reading of the decision and labels it as one reading, then says
which test he prefers and discloses that he argued for it himself. What is
settled and what is left to lower courts sit in separate sentences. The stake
is disclosed in the same sentence as the preference, and that is where the
writer is visible.

## Daphne Keller, "The DSA's Industrial Model for Content Moderation"

Source: https://verfassungsblog.de/dsa-industrial-model/

> "The DSA’s mandated process includes notice, appeals, out-of-court dispute
> resolution, and formal reporting for each item of content removed. Platforms
> must notify the affected user, conveying “a clear and specific statement of
> reasons” with multiple enumerated elements (Art. 15). The users who receive
> these notices can contest the decisions, seeking new review by the platform
> (Art. 17). They can also bring the dispute to new out-of-court adjudication
> bodies for another round of review, at platform expense (Art. 18).
> (Platforms pay their own fees and expenses regardless of the case’s outcome.
> If they lose, they also pay the user’s fees and expenses.)"

One sentence names the whole process, then each duty gets a sentence of its own
with the article number closing it, and the regulation is quoted for one phrase
only, the one a platform would have to satisfy word for word. The parenthetical
answers who pays, which the Articles do not put in one place. Choosing that as
the detail worth the aside is a practitioner's choice, and it is hers.

> "Importantly, all this process is expensive. Particularly for smaller
> platforms, it will require substantial new engineering and product design
> efforts, and hiring considerably more content moderators. The way to avoid
> that expense will be to moderate less. Platforms that remove only illegal
> content, and don’t use their Terms of Service (TOS) to impose more expansive
> rules, will save a lot of money. So will platforms that choose not to employ
> automated content moderation tools like upload filters, since those will more
> frequently trigger the DSA’s costly processes."

Each sentence follows from the one before it: the process costs money, the way
to avoid the cost is to moderate less, and here are the two kinds of platform
that would save the most. The prediction is stated as her own, and the reader
can check every step against the duties she laid out earlier. Nothing in it is
attributed to unnamed observers.
